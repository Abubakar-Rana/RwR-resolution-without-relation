Tile Selection Helps Perception but Hurts
Spatial Reasoning in Ultra-High-Resolution
Remote Sensing Visual Question Answering
Anonymous Author(s)
Aﬃliation withheld for review
Abstract. Vision-language models (VLMs) must downscale remote
sensing scenes of ultra-high resolution (UHR), so a common remedy is
to select tiles relevant to the question and reason over them at native
resolution. We asked whether this loses the spatial relations between ob-
jects in diﬀerent tiles, and whether restoring them explicitly helps. We
built a hierarchical pipeline that registers objects from selected tiles in
a global coordinate frame and computes a geometric relation graph, and
compared it with three baselines (whole-image input, independent tiles,
and tile selection without relations) on 3,080 XLRS-Bench items using
Qwen2.5-VL-7B. The comparison is paired, and the key contrast holds
VLM calls and encoded pixels ﬁxed. The explicit relations did not help:
the change on relational tasks was +0.0 pp (95% CI [ −2.1, +2.1]). The
experiment instead shows a signiﬁcant crossover. Tile selection raises
accuracy on perception questions by +4.1 pp but lowers it on relational
questions by −4.5 pp (interaction −8.6 pp, p < 10−4), which puts object
spatial relationships near chance. We trace the null result to the step
before the relations: the VLM used as an object extractor returned no
objects for 66% of scenes. The results point to routing between global
and local views, and to more reliable grounding, rather than to richer
relation encodings.
Keywords: Remote sensing · Vision-language models · Ultra-high res-
olution · Spatial reasoning
1 Introduction
UHR remote sensing images are 8,500 ×8,500 pixels or larger. XLRS-Bench [ 19]
made plain what this means for VLMs: a target object may cover a few hundred
pixels, and whole-image encoding [ 1] downscales it away. The ﬁeld’s answer is to
zoom, whether by searching [ 21,16], pruning tokens [ 11], retrieving regions [ 24],
or learning an active crop policy [ 10], each trading global context for local detail.
We tested a speciﬁc concern with that trade. When evidence is spread across
tiles, the model sees each crop in isolation and may lose the spatial relations
between them. We therefore built an explicit remedy (Fig. 1). Objects extracted
from the selected tiles are lifted into one global pixel frame and deduplicated
across tile seams. A geometric relation graph (direction, distance, adjacency,

– a tightly bounded null for explicit relations ( ±2 pp), traced to grounding:
extraction returned no objects for 66% of scenes.
2 Related Work
High-resolution VLMs. Tiling encoders such as Monkey [ 8] and LLaV A-
UHD [ 22], and native dynamic-resolution models such as Qwen2.5-VL [ 1], raise
the input pixel budget. Training-free methods add search or hierarchy at in-
ference time: V* [ 21], ZoomEye [ 16], and DC 2 [20]. UHR remote sensing.
XLRS-Bench [19] and GeoLLaV A-8K [18] target 8K-scale scenes. LRS-VQA [ 11]
prunes tokens under text guidance, ImageRAG [ 24] retrieves relevant regions,
and ZoomEarth [ 10] learns an active zoom policy. UHR-Micro [ 13] documents
a “resolution illusion” for small evidence; GeoChat [ 7] is a domain-tuned VLM.
Spatial reasoning and structured intermediates. VLMs are weak at spatial
relations [ 2,15]. Scene graphs [ 6], visual programs [ 4,17], and visual marks [ 23]
externalise that structure; we test it for cross-tile relations under matched com-
pute.
3 Method
Selection. Each scene is cut into 1024-pixel tiles with 128 pixels of overlap (93
tiles per scene on average). Tiles are scored by 0.35 ssal + 0.65 sclip. Here ssal is
gradient and variance energy on a downscaled overview, and sclip is the CLIP [ 14]
similarity between the question and each tile thumbnail. The top-5 tiles are kept,
suppressing near-duplicate neighbours. Local extraction and registration.
For each selected crop, the VLM returns JSON-formatted objects with boxes on
a 0–1000 grid over the crop, which we lift to global pixels in code. Detections
are then merged across tiles by a label-aware greedy rule (IoU, containment,
or near-touching boxes at a seam), which yields at most 60 objects per scene.
Relations. For each object and its 6 nearest neighbours, we emit one predicate
per pair, in priority order: containment (intersection over the smaller box ≥0.85);
intersection (IoU ≥0.05 or overlap ≥5% of the smaller box, e.g. roads crossing
rivers); adjacency (border gap ≤1.2% of the image diagonal); otherwise 8-way

4 Experimental Setup
Data. We use the XLRS-Bench-lite split [ 19]: 3,080 four-option multiple-choice
items (chance level 25%) in 13 categories. 97 of the 100 Overall-Land-Use items
are multi-select (e.g. answer “BCD”) and were excluded from every method, leav-
ing 2,983 scored items. Before the experiment we ﬁxed the relational group: ob-
ject spatial relationship, regional counting, counting with complex reasoning, and
route planning (800 items). The remaining 2,123 items form the non-relational
group. A 60-item bi-temporal category not matched by our taxonomy is held
out of both groups but counted overall. Model and budget. We used Qwen2.5-
VL-7B-Instruct [ 1] in bf16 with greedy decoding and a budget of 1,280 visual
tokens per image, on one NVIDIA L40S (35.6 GPU-hours in total). Statistics.
All comparisons are paired on items. We use McNemar’s exact test [ 12] and
10,000-sample paired bootstrap conﬁdence intervals [ 3]. Per-task tests are Holm-
corrected [ 5]. The two group-level Selection vs Proposed tests are conﬁrmatory,
and all other analyses are exploratory.

5.3 Why explicit relations did not help
The relation graph was usually empty . On 66% of scenes, the VLM extrac-
tor returned no parseable object in any of the ﬁve selected crops (Fig. 4a). For
object colour, classiﬁcation and motion state, the rate was 98–100%. Only 21%
of scenes produced any cross-tile relation. When objects were found, graphs were
rich (16.3 objects and 59.3 relations on average), and parsing was not the cause
(Appendix A.4). When relations were present, the model used them,
and the eﬀect depended on the task group. We use the rate at which the
answer changes between Selection and Proposed as a probe. It is 5.6% when the
relation block is empty; this is the noise ﬂoor from the prompt text alone. It rises
to 17.4% when relations are present, and to 30.0% on relational items with cross-
tile edges (Fig. 4b). The sign of the eﬀect followed our hypothesis in direction
but was not signiﬁcant: +2.8 pp [ −2.0, +7.7] on relational items with cross-tile
relations ( n = 247 ), and −4.1 pp [ −7.5, −1.0] on non-relational ones ( n = 293 ;
uncorrected p = 0 .02). On perception questions the relations therefore act as
distractors (post-treatment strata; indicative only). The rise of accuracy with
object count is not evidence for the method: it appears in Direct as well, which
never sees any objects (Appendix, Fig. 7). Object-rich scenes simply belong to
easier tasks.

5.4 Complementarity and cost
Direct and Selection fail on diﬀerent items. An oracle that picks the correct
one of the two reaches 51.2% overall (44.9% relational, 53.6% non-relational),
against 43.1% for the best single method. Selection and Proposed cost 5.6 × the
wall time of Direct, and local extraction accounts for 77% of that time, while
their net gain is +1.8 pp. A router that decides whether to zoom could capture
most of the complementarity at a fraction of the cost.
6 Discussion and Conclusion
Under matched compute, explicit cross-tile relations did not improve UHR re-
mote sensing VQA. Two lessons follow. First, zoom-based pipelines trade rela-
tional accuracy for perceptual accuracy, and aggregate scores hide the trade, so
relational and perception tasks should be reported separately. Second, a sym-
bolic relation layer is only as good as its grounding: a 7B VLM prompted as
a detector returned nothing on most scenes, precisely on the small-object ques-
tions that motivate zooming. A dedicated detector is the necessary next test.
Limitations: one 7B model, the multiple-choice lite split only, a generic CLIP
scorer [ 9], and a relational partition of our own design (Appendix A.2).

A.2 Further caveats
The planned ablation sweeps over tile size, overlap and top- k were not run, so
the conﬁguration reported here is a single operating point. All methods chose
option “C” for 37–41% of items, against 27% of ground-truth answers: a posi-
tion bias shared across methods, which aﬀects absolute accuracy but not the
paired comparisons. The 97 multi-select Overall-Land-Use items were excluded
identically from every method; set-based scoring of these items is left to future
work. The stratiﬁed analysis in Section 5.3 conditions on the number of relations,
which is a post-treatment variable, and its p-values are uncorrected.
